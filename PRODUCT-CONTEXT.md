# Let’s Code — approved product context

## Purpose and identity
Let’s Code helps programmers arrive alone and leave with a team ready for a funded challenge. It supports Mark Kendall’s Intent Driven Saturdays and Proof Before Resume initiative.

Working product line: **Find your team. Build your proof.**

Intent Driven Saturdays slogan: **Build Saturday. Win Money. Bring the Learning Back to Your Corporation on Monday.**

Proof Before Resume positioning: **Stop screening claims. Start evaluating evidence.**

Let’s Code is a working product name. No domain ownership or name availability has been established. Public-facing copy should describe its own purpose and experience; do not include competitor comparisons or an inspiration attribution.

## Experience principles
Give people an understandable opportunity immediately. Ask for very little. Skills and pictures make people discoverable; a four-seat carousel makes the emerging team visible. Invitation acceptance creates the team. Participants arrange their work externally using contact details they choose to share. Quiet participation, useful search, and a clear commitment matter more than social features.

The intended appearance is professional, welcoming, and energetic. Let Mark judge the running result. Avoid exaggerated earnings promises, a crowded feed, fabricated activity counts, or messaging that resembles a recruitment scheme.

## Commercial direction — context for later iterations
Programmers participate free. Employers, recruiters, and technology sponsors may fund events. The proposed sponsorship range is $7,000–$10,000, potentially including a $5,000 prize. These are working assumptions, not validated pricing or a live offer. Prize funding, event operations, and any later code purchase must be explicit and distinct.

The event sells access to demonstrated outcomes and independent ideas. It does not sell participants’ email addresses or unrestricted repository access. Recruiting introductions require participant agreement.

Keep separate counts for registered programmers, event opt-ins, completed teams, checked-in teams, and valid submissions. Registrations alone do not guarantee attendance. No current audience size, sponsor commitment, or participation rate has been established.

## Challenge and evidence model — later scope
The proposed event is four hours, with four-person teams and four judges. Every team receives the same official requirements, start time, permitted tools, and scoring criteria. A common advance preview may help team formation; do not give selected teams privileged requirements.

Teams retain ownership and keep code private. Judges receive limited review access. Sponsors see outputs, demos, findings, and validation rather than source code. The first shortlist can contain four teams; further review rounds must fit the agreed event scope and preserve team consent. Code purchase or licensing is a separate agreement with its owners; a prize does not automatically transfer ownership.

Use bounded, representative, synthetic or sanitized challenges. Do not present a four-hour prototype as production-ready. Before a real event, clarify sponsor deliverables, minimum participation, cancellation terms, prize handling, judging access, and the optional acquisition process.

## Why V1 is deliberately small
First prove that an individual can find complementary people and form a team with little effort. Later iterations can add real multi-user operation, event administration, check-in, submission evidence, private judging, and sponsor views in response to observed needs. They are not part of the first build.
